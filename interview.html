A Software Engineer's Opinion on Technology

Have we reached a technology epidemic?

Conducted by Emeralda Jin on April 12, 2026

For Laurel Schwulst's Visual Principles for the Screen at Washington University in St. Louis.

Chad Myers is a Boeing software engineer from Springfield, MO. He double-majored in Computer Science and English Literature at Truman State, and is currently pursuing a Masters in Electrical Engineering at WashU.

Q1: You double-majored in English and Engineering. Are there any aspects of your English degree that influenced your engineering career?

I think that one thing that engineers get typified as is that they just lack communication skills. So I think being able to dissect ideas in a way that only the humanities can offer is actually a big asset for the engineering process.

So, like, you would say that you gain communication skills?

Yeah, communication skills. Okay. Writing skills too. Lots of technical, we have to maintain a ton of technical documentation stuff. So whenever we need to update that, then it helps to have someone who has things like reading and writing skills.

"...being able to dissect ideas in a way that only the humanities can offer is actually a big asset for the engineering process."

Q2: What does a day as a software engineer look like for you? Are there any things you don't like about your job?

Um, yeah, I mean, honestly, it's just a lot of meetings and a lot of just combing through really old code.

Are you guys fixing up the old code?

Um, yes and no. So basically, as you start to build up a software application, you're usually working on a pre-existing application, which means that, there's a lot of just things that could have just been designed better. There are lots of updates whenever you update like a dot net version or something. Those, uh, you have to update all of those changes. And then different needs are just found to be necessary. I'm also working on a new application right now, so I'm working on that from scratch, but it's just a complete rebuild of a pre-existing application.

"You're usually working on a pre-existing application, which means that, there's a lot of just things that could have just been designed better."

Q3: What are some valuable skills you learned from software engineering?

I think that being an engineer is a lot of being able to break down ideas into manageable chunks. And so I feel like the main skills that I got from engineering were just being able to break down a process into a way that allows me to actually be able to contribute something. Since a lot of these applications are just massive nowadays, it's kind of hard to just get rolling on that, so I feel like my time studying computer science really enabled me to get a good understanding of how software is structured, how to break down software, basically how we organize our code, that sort of thing.

So organization and starting with the core components.

Yeah, you know, like being able to, I guess, take something and then find a way to make it a little bit better. I mean, one of the big things about software is that it's like, easily scalable. Because with other types of engineering, you have to have a lot of money. If I wanted to open a napkin factory or something, then that would be like still, like 100s of 1000s of dollars. But with software, it's just to a large extent, if you have a decent computer, you can kind of make whatever. It's really only limited by your creativity.

So, you think coding is very accessible then?

I would say more so than the other fields of engineering. Yes.

"I feel like my time studying computer science really enabled me to get a good understanding of how software is structured, how to break down software, basically how we organize our code, that sort of thing. "

Q4: Do you have a preference for e-books or traditional books and why?

Oh, traditional, all the way. I don't think there's a reason why?

Like, does it just feel nicer?

Well, yes and no. Part of it is because whenever I buy a book, I own the physical copy of it. If I buy an e-book, I purchase the rights to the book, which feels a little different. So, I mean, I think that there's something to be said about having access to physical media, as opposed to digital. Like, for instance, you know, if I wanted to get a physical copy of the book, I could just go to the library, I guess, but I mean, you know, the wait times on certain books can take a while. I also just like the paper. I like annotating a lot too. It's nice to see how my ideas evolve over time with certain books. You know, books that are in high school that I have little annotations on, and then you can kind of see how your ideas evolve over time.

"Traditional... It's nice to see how my ideas evolve over time with certain books. You know, books that are in high school that I have little annotations on, and then you can kind of see how your ideas evolve over time."

Q5: What's your guideline for what should be digital and what should be traditional?

Um, that is a good question. I mean, really, I feel like it's kind of a personal preference thing and inherently kind of nebulous. But really, if it makes my life easier, then I say that I would rather just go traditional. If it makes my life harder, then it should go. If it makes my life easier than it should go digital, but otherwise, I think that we really should just stick with traditional. Sometimes technology makes things harder and not everyone gets that. Because by introducing technology in a place where it's unnecessary, it just creates more points of failure.

"Sometimes technology makes things harder and not everyone gets that. Because by introducing technology in a place where it's unnecessary, it just creates more points of failure. "

Q6: Do you remember when you were 1st introduced to screens?

I remember my parents wouldn't let me play normal video games. But I played this computer game from the 90s.

Do you remember the name of it?

Um, Botley's Adventures on Mystery Mountain. The Ed Tech games were so fun. My parents would actually check that out pretty frequently from the library and stuff. We'd check out the individual CD ROMs and sometimes it would have multiple CDs that you would have to load up in order to get the game to activate. I remember the original—not the original Oregon trail, but like the one I played. You had to have like 2 or 3 separate disks.

"Botley's Adventures on Mystery Mountain."

Q7: How do you think your relationship with screens has changed since then?

I just feel like I'm always looking at a screen, like, it's almost like noise, if that makes any sense.

Wait, elaborate on that, because we're actually doing a class project that's related to noise.

I don't know. It's just, like, it's always just kind of there. Like people expect me to be accessible all the time. You know, with work from home, I feel like I have a unique position of not really being able to separate my work from like my home life either. So, it kind of just feels like the more I try to get away from screens, the more like screens keep on trying to pervade the rest of my existence.

"...it kind of just feels like the more I try to get away from screens, the more like screens keep on trying to pervade the rest of my existence."

Q8: Then, are there any things that you wish could be digital and are there any things that you wish weren't digital?

Honestly, I don't think there are more things that I want to be digital. I know that sounds like a weird thing, but I feel like it kind of hit like the inflection point. I feel like there's—it's kind of the terrible thing to say as a software engineer, but... I feel like a lot of the processes and stuff that we were going to automate we already have. Like, I just don't know how much more technology is really going to help us.

Whenever we start automating everything, then, you know, the whole idea was that we would be able to work fewer hours and increase productivity. And clearly that didn't really work out. And so I feel like a lot of the promises that were given to us by tech ended up accidentally just kind of accelerating. Like some pretty glaring problems with, like, for example, how we treat artists and the indust, and like the entertainment industry. And I don't know.

It's just like, look at AI and like AI generated art now. I mean, we're using that as a tool to undermine artists and their labor. And so we're just losing the human element of it progressively. Now, in terms of things that I wish should definitely be traditional, I do think art should not uh, things like AI and like digital technology. Like digital art is an amazing, wonderful tool. AI art. No, I want it as far away from like... creative work as possible.

"I feel like we've hit an inflection point... I just don't know how much more technology is really going to help us."

"...the whole idea was that we would be able to work fewer hours and increase productivity. And clearly that didn't really work out."
